FoulCast

How this works

Every page here does two things: it quotes what the club publishes about where its protective netting runs, and it estimates how many foul balls a game reach each area of seats. The first is sourced. The second is a model.

The model has never been checked against a real foul ball

There is no public record of where foul balls actually land — Statcast logs that a foul happened, not where it came down. So the distributions on these pages are estimates from physics and published park dimensions, and no page here puts a number on how often the model gets it right, because there is nothing to compute one from. Every figure on every park page is a physics model that has never been validated against an observed landing, and the line under each table — model estimate, not observed data — is the short form of this paragraph.

What is sourced and what is not

Netting is sourced, and quoted rather than interpreted. Each park page quotes what the club itself publishes about where its netting runs, word for word, and links to the page the words came from. The clubs' pages were first read on 2026-08-09 and read again for the quotations on 2026-10-20. At 25 of the 31 parks the club says where its netting runs; at one its page says two things that cannot both be true, and both are quoted; at the other five nothing the club publishes says where it runs.

No page says which of this model's own seating areas the netting stands in front of. Most clubs give section numbers, and putting those onto this model's areas means reading them against the seat labels this model carries — which the parks' own seating maps show are in the wrong place at twenty-seven of the thirty parks whose map has been read. So the club's sentence is given as it stands, and a reader holding a ticket with a section number on it can make the one join this site is not in a position to make.

The park dimensions are sourced — foul territory area and backstop distance, from Andrew Clem's stadium statistics, cross-checked against the Seamheads ballpark database, with the disagreements between them stated on each page. They place each park's seating. They do not shape it: no public source gives the angle of a seating area off the foul line or the height of a deck in feet, for any ballpark, so every park here shares one bowl shape and every park is modelled as an exact left-right mirror.

One consequence is visible from the grid of drawings and worth saying out loud: the seats behind home plate come out busiest at 8 of the 31 parks here. That is partly a real effect — balls deflected back over the catcher have to land somewhere, and it is why there is a screen there — and partly an artefact of every park sharing one bowl shape. It is not a finding about any individual ballpark.

What a club's netting statement means

Read the clubs literally: there is "some amount of netting or screening" in front of the listed seats, its "height and coverage will vary by section", and fans behind it "are still exposed to objects leaving the field of play". Behind netting is not fully protected, and no seat here is described as protected outright.

No seat on this site is described as protected outright, and none of them is called a good bet either. The clubs' own netting pages say fans sitting behind netting "are still exposed to objects leaving the field of play", and this model has never seen a real foul ball.

Which foul line is which, and why most pages will not say

Every ballpark on this site is modelled as an exact left-right mirror, which means a park with its two sides written down the wrong way round produces figures identical to one with them the right way round. This model cannot see the difference from the inside, and neither can a published netting range: it gives the two ends of the run, not which foul line each end is on. Only a source that names a side alongside specific seats can settle it, and 16 of the 31 parks have one.

This is not a hypothetical. At one ballpark the two sides were written down backwards, and its netting was marked area by area on this site, cited and apparently matched, until its own seating map was read. So at the parks with nothing to check against, the two foul lines are shown as a single seating area each and no area is called first-base or third-base. Every park page states its own position on this, under Details, whichever of the four it is in. A club's own words may still name a foul line on such a page: they name the club's sections, not this model's areas.

Why there are no section numbers here, outside a quotation

This model carries printed seat labels for every area it tracks. The first check on them was against the clubs' netting pages alone: nine parks' labels are contradicted outright there, and ten more cannot describe a continuous seating bowl. Every seating map this project holds has since been read directly, at magnification, and compared label by label — a far stronger check, and one the netting pages could not make. Thirty maps, covering thirty of the 31 parks, and twenty-seven of them disagreed with the model.

Four ballparks have their two foul lines cleanly reversed, and at nine more the two lines are crossed in a way no single swap would fix — an area the model calls third base sitting out in right field, or a ballpark that numbers one foul line odd and the other even, so every area the model draws there is half one line and half the other. At twenty-four the seats behind home plate are not the seats this model calls the seats behind home plate, in one case eighteen positions away at a corner of the ballpark. Several name whole decks that are not in the building. Each of the twenty-seven park pages says so in a line under its table.

Three ballparks agreed, on both questions a map can settle. They are stated on their own pages in the same place and at the same length as the twenty-seven, because they were checked the same way, and an agreement nobody publishes reads as an absence of evidence. The thirty-first park has no map to read at all, and its page says so. So seating is described by position — the lower bowl behind the plate, the dugout boxes down the foul lines — and the only section numbers anywhere on this site are inside a quotation from a club, where they are the club's own numbers for its own seats and carry none of this model's errors.

Reading the drawing and the table

Home plate at the bottom, the two foul lines running out, and the seating areas the model tracks drawn as bands of an arc behind them. It is a schematic, not to scale and not a seating chart: one generic bowl, its depth set by each park's published foul territory and backstop. Every park is drawn in the same feet, so a park with more published foul ground really is a larger drawing — the grid on the front page is where that shows, because it is the one place the 31 are side by side. A park's own page compares it with nothing and crops the same drawing to that park. The drawing carries no netting: the club's own sentence, directly under it, is where that is.

Shade carries the model's figures, in four bands: Most More Fewer Fewest. They are bands against the busiest area at the same park — "most" is at least three quarters of that area's figure, "more" at least half, "fewer" at least a quarter, "fewest" anything less — and the table under each drawing gives every area's band in the same words, beside a swatch of the same shade. "None" is an area the model sends no ball to at all: at one park, a whole deck under a roof. The figures the bands are drawn from are on each park page under Details, and nowhere above it. Both foul lines are always shaded alike: the model builds them as exact mirrors, so the gap between the two figures is simulation noise.

The bands are relative to the park and not fixed across the 31, because the parks barely differ. The model puts the same number of fouls into every game, most parks put within a few of one another into seats, and what difference there is comes mostly from the one bowl shape they all share. Shading the parks against one another would compare that shape with itself; within one park, which areas the model sends more balls to than others is the thing it does say.

No figure on this site is ever drawn as a length — no bars, no filled shares, no meters. The numbers come out of estimated geometry that has never been compared with a foul ball, and a drawn length would put them on a scale and say they were measured to it. The drawing's own lengths are the two sourced measurements; the model's figures are carried by shade and by a word, because the run cannot resolve the difference between 2.1 and 2.3 fouls a game and neither a smooth ramp nor a figure printed to a tenth of a ball should say it could.

How the figures were produced

One full game, both lineups, the same 18 batters at every park on this site, so the park is the only thing that changes. The eighteen are real hitters chosen to stand for the league rather than for any club: nine from each side of the plate, one from each ninth of the league’s range of fouls per plate appearance, each the hitter in that ninth whose exit speed, launch angle and pull tendency on Statcast for 2025–26 sit nearest the league average. Those figures, the hitter’s own, are what the model samples from. 400 simulations per batter, fixed seed, run on 2026-10-21. The table on each park page gives each area's band, busiest first; the figures behind the bands, in foul balls a game, are under Details — a model estimate, not a count of anything observed.

A share of the fouls the model produces land where it has no seating area to put them — deep down the lines near the poles, in the gap in front of the first row, or under a covered deck. They are counted in the park's total and then dropped, and each park page states its own share, which is why the figures are a shape rather than a census.

At a park where nothing says which foul line is which, the two foul lines are one area each rather than a first-base area and a third-base one. A figure on one of those rows is what reaches one of the two lines, which keeps it comparable with the behind-plate rows beside it. The model builds both lines identically, so the pair would differ only by simulation noise in any case.

What this model does not know

No foul ball has ever been checked against it

This model has never been compared with a record of where real foul balls landed, at this park or any other, and no such record exists publicly — Statcast logs that a foul happened, not where it came down. The largest hand-collected set anyone has published is FiveThirtyEight's 906 fouls, one game at each of ten parks, placed by eye off camera footage into broad zones. Everything here is an estimate from physics and published dimensions.

The seating shape is not surveyed

The park's published foul territory and backstop figures set how far each area sits from home plate. The angles and heights are published nowhere, for any ballpark, so every park here shares one bowl shape, scaled and placed by its own measurements. Every park is also modelled as an exact left-right mirror, which one club's published statement about its own lopsided foul ground says is wrong.

Some fouls land where the model has no seats

A substantial share come down where there is no seating area to put them — deep near the poles, in the gap in front of the first row, or under a covered deck. They are counted in the park total and then dropped. The share is stated on this page, and it is large enough that these figures are a shape, not a census.

The rate of balls hit straight back is a guess

Roughly a quarter to a third of the fouls here are deflections carrying back over the catcher, which is what fills the seats behind the plate. The rate driving that was chosen because it produces a plausible game, not because anything measured it. It is the single largest unvalidated number in the model, and it lands exactly there.

The ball flies with less lift than a real one

The flight model gives a spinning ball about a fifth of the lift that wind-tunnel studies of a baseball measure, and makes up for it with more drag, because the two were fitted together to Statcast's own distance figure. The result is that each ball's distance is close to that figure — long by about fifteen feet on average over six thousand tracked fouls — while its arc is flatter and lower than a real foul's, which decides whether it clears a deck's front edge. Raising the lift would need the drag refitted against the same data, and that has not been done. The direction of the lift was wrong until Step 27 and is now perpendicular to the ball's path, as it should be.

One foul line is not distinguishable from the other

The mirror leaves the two lines at a park differing only by simulation noise, so read a matching pair of areas as one number rather than two. Worse, at 15 of the 31 parks nothing available establishes which of the two lines is which — and because the mirror makes a reversed park produce figures identical to a correct one, this model cannot detect the difference from the inside. It went undetected at one park for the whole of this model's life until the ballpark's own map was read.
